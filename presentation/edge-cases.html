💡 Proposed Solution: 5-Review Threshold

  • Hide ratings until 5+ reviews: Prevents misleading averages from 1-2 outlier reviews
  • Statistical validity: Minimum sample size needed for meaningful average that users can trust
  • Industry standard: Amazon, Yelp, and major platforms use 3-10 review thresholds before displaying ratings
  • Trust signal: Users inherently distrust "5.0★ (2 reviews)" but trust "4.5★ (234 reviews)"
  • Reduces gaming risk: Harder to manipulate ratings when threshold requires multiple reviews

❓ Open Questions

5 vs 10 threshold: Is 5 reviews enough for HelloFresh context, or should we require 10+ for higher confidence?
Low rating intervention: If a recipe hits 5+ reviews with poor rating (2-3★), do we investigate recipe quality or menu placement?

⚠️ Premium Recipe Upselling Risk

Business Risk: Premium recipes cost €3-5 more than standard meals. If ratings are visible and a premium recipe has a low rating, it creates conversion friction at the point of upsell.

💡 Why Personal First

  • Individual taste matters more: A 5★ personal rating is more predictive of future satisfaction than community consensus
  • Builds trust in recommendations: Surfacing recipes users already love reinforces that we understand their preferences
  • Encourages exploration: Users may discover they like recipes the broader community rated lower

❓ Checking for Concerns

User confusion: Could showing "Your top rated" with a low community rating confuse users or make them question their own taste?
Social proof conflict: Does highlighting personal preference over social proof reduce the value of community ratings?

3. Market-Specific Ratings (For Discussion)

Strategic Question: Same recipe in different markets (Germany vs Netherlands) may have different ratings due to local taste preferences or ingredient sourcing. Should we use global data or keep ratings market-specific?

❓ Key Decisions

Global vs local data: Should we show global rating data aggregated across all markets, or keep ratings strictly market-specific?
Pattern analysis: Can we identify patterns showing when ratings differ significantly between markets? Is this driven by ingredient quality, taste preferences, or recipe execution differences?
Volume-based strategy: Should the decision depend on rating volume? For example: use market-specific when we have 20+ local reviews, but fall back to global data when volume is low?
Data validation: Do we have enough historical data to determine if market differences are statistically significant or just noise from small sample sizes?

4. Suspicious Rating Patterns

Proposed Approach: For now, show a curated subset of ratings rather than all reviews. This allows us to filter out suspicious patterns, maintain quality control, and present the most helpful ratings first. We won't display the full review list yet — focus on aggregate ratings plus a tailored selection of representative reviews.

💡 Why Tailored Subset First

  • Quality over quantity: Surface the most helpful and verified reviews instead of overwhelming users with volume
  • Spam protection: Gives us time to implement detection systems before exposing full review streams
  • Gradual rollout: Start with aggregate ratings + curated subset, then expand to full reviews once moderation is proven
  • Learning phase: Allows us to understand rating patterns and user behavior before committing to full transparency

❓ Open Questions

Selection criteria: What makes a review "helpful" enough to show in the subset? (verification status, helpfulness votes, detailed feedback)
Subset size: Currently the subset is 10 reviews. Should we show more based on total volume?
