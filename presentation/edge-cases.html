Edge Cases & Exceptional States

Handling scenarios where community ratings data is limited, absent, or requires special treatment

1. Recommendation Contexts

The Edge Case: When we display community ratings, low-rated recipes (e.g., 2.5★) may appear in high-visibility recommendation contexts like "Top 20 This Week", "For You" swimlanes, or preselected meals. This creates cognitive dissonance: users expect "recommended" recipes to be high quality, but the rating suggests otherwise.

The Problem

  • User sees "Top 20" or preselected meal with 2.5★ recipe → Erodes trust in HelloFresh curation
  • Creates disconnect: "Why is HelloFresh recommending poorly-rated recipes?"
  • Recommendations are based on order volume, freshness, business goals — not just quality
  • A highly-ordered recipe (popular) can still be poorly-rated (disappointing experience)

3. Upselling

The Edge Case: Premium recipes cost €3-5 more than standard meals. If ratings are visible and a premium recipe has a low rating, it creates conversion friction at the point of upsell. Should we treat premium recipe ratings differently than standard recipes?

Considerations

  • Premium recipes have smaller order volumes → slower to reach review thresholds
  • Higher price point = higher expectations → ratings may skew lower relative to standard recipes
  • Low ratings on premium could discourage upsells and impact revenue

Strategy Questions

  • Different threshold: Should premium recipes require 10+ reviews before showing ratings (vs 5 for standard)?
  • Quality trigger: If premium hits 3.5★ or below with 10+ reviews, investigate or remove from menu?
  • Unified vs separate: Show premium and standard ratings separately, or combine into one pool?

Recommendation: Use same 5-review threshold for M1, but monitor premium conversion rates. If low-rated premiums impact revenue, consider higher threshold (10 reviews) or quality triggers in M2.

Archived: Market-Specific Ratings

The Edge Case: Same recipe in different markets (Germany vs Netherlands) may have different ratings due to local taste preferences or ingredient sourcing. Should we show global aggregated data or keep ratings market-specific?

Solution Option 1: Global Aggregated Ratings

Approach: Combine all ratings across all markets. Show single global average for each recipe.

Pros

  • Higher rating volume — recipes reach threshold faster
  • Simpler data infrastructure (one rating pool per recipe)

Cons

  • May not reflect local ingredient quality or taste preferences
  • German users see ratings from Dutch/Belgian users with different context

Solution Option 2: Market-Specific Ratings Proposed

Approach: Keep ratings separate per market. German users only see German ratings, Dutch see Dutch ratings.

Pros

  • More relevant to local context (ingredient sourcing, taste preferences)
  • Reflects local execution quality (fulfillment center differences)

Cons

  • Lower rating volume per market — takes longer to reach threshold
  • More complex data infrastructure (separate pools per market)

Solution Option 3: Hybrid (Volume-Based Fallback)

Approach: Use market-specific ratings when available (10+ reviews). Fall back to global when market-specific volume is low.

Pros

  • Balances relevance (local when possible) with volume (global as backup)

Cons

  • Inconsistent logic — users may not understand why rating changes
  • More complex to implement and maintain

Our proposal is Option 2 - Market-Specific Ratings. Local relevance is more valuable than higher volume. Ingredient quality and taste preferences vary significantly by market. German users should see German experiences. Start market-specific in M1, monitor if rating volume is sufficient, and can revisit global fallback in M2 if needed.

Archived: Suspicious Rating Patterns

The Edge Case: As ratings grow, we may encounter spam, fake reviews, or manipulation attempts. How do we protect rating integrity while maintaining transparency and user trust?

Solution Option 1: Show All Reviews (Full Transparency)

Approach: Display every review submitted. No filtering or curation.

Pros

  • Maximum transparency — users see everything

Cons

  • Spam and fake reviews pollute the feed
  • No quality control — unhelpful reviews dominate
  • Vulnerable to manipulation (competitors, bad actors)

Solution Option 2: Curated Subset Proposed

Approach: Show aggregate rating (all reviews count) but display curated subset of ~10 most helpful reviews. Filter out suspicious patterns.

Pros

  • Quality over quantity — surface helpful, verified reviews first
  • Spam protection without full moderation infrastructure (gradual rollout)
  • Learning phase — understand patterns before committing to full transparency

Cons

  • Less transparent — not all reviews are visible

Solution Option 3: Heavy Moderation + Report System

Approach: Show all reviews but implement strong moderation tools, user reporting, and "helpful" voting.

Pros

  • Full transparency with safety nets

Cons

  • Requires significant moderation resources (team, tools, processes)
  • Slower to market — need to build moderation infrastructure first

Our proposal is Option 2 - Curated Subset. Start with curated subset in M1 to protect quality during initial rollout. Build moderation tools in background. Expand to more reviews in M2, then full transparency with reporting in M3 once we understand rating patterns and have proper safety measures.
